# Executable Cinema: The Bell Labs Computer Movie Lineage and the Genesis of Computational Image Media (1963--1968)

## Introduction: The Epistemic Shift Toward Operative Ekphrasis

Between the pivotal years of 1963 and 1968, the unique research
environment at Bell Telephone Laboratories fundamentally altered the
ontological status of the motion picture. The institutional substrate of
Bell Labs did not merely apply computers to the pre-existing medium of
film; rather, it transformed film into an executable technical
object.^1^ This period marks a profound media-archaeological rupture.
The motion picture evolved from a photographically captured record of
physical reality---reliant on the physics of optics and
photochemistry---into an algorithmic manifestation of programmed logic.
During this narrow but explosive window of innovation, calculation
became image, image became motion, and motion became what can be termed
a \"worldtext\"---a visual space governed entirely by operative
linguistic commands.

The locus of this transformation resides in a specific cluster of
technological innovations, programmatic paradigms, and
cross-disciplinary collaborations that emerged within the confines of
Bell Labs in Murray Hill, New Jersey.^1^ The lineage connecting these
innovations traces a trajectory from pure mathematics to avant-garde
poetry. It binds Edward E. Zajac's pioneering scientific simulation
animations to Kenneth C. Knowlton's invention of BEFLIX, the world\'s
first dedicated computer animation programming language.^1^ It extends
into Knowlton and Leon Harmon's groundbreaking explorations into
gray-scale image synthesis, which redefined the limits of human visual
perception.^4^ Finally, it tracks the subsequent migration of these
computational tools into experimental cinema through the collaborative
*Poemfield* series produced by Stan VanDerBeek and Knowlton.^6^

To rigorously trace this lineage is to observe how computer programs
became instruments not just for calculation, but for producing motion,
perception, image, simulation, and poetic cinema. In Zajac's work, the
movie serves as a scientific simulation made visible. It functioned as
an elegant solution to the epistemic bottleneck of interpreting vast
matrices of numerical data regarding satellite orbital dynamics.^9^ In
Knowlton's software engineering, the movie becomes a programmable
sequence of bitmap operations, fundamentally redefining animation as the
manipulation of a discrete Cartesian grid through algorithmic
subroutines.^1^

In the perceptual collaborations between Harmon and Knowlton, the
discrete, binary logic of the computer is mapped directly onto the
psychophysics of human vision, turning gray-scale synthesis into a
cognitive experiment that inadvertently birthed one of the most famous
pieces of early digital art.^4^ Finally, in VanDerBeek and Knowlton's
cinematic collaborations, executable cinema achieves an avant-garde
synthesis, turning the output of an IBM 7094 mainframe and a
Stromberg-Carlson 4020 microfilm recorder into an algorithmic,
typographic dreamscape.^6^

This exhaustive research report reconstructs the verified, annotated
research map of Bell Labs computer movie sources, detailing the precise
technical, mathematical, and aesthetic mechanisms that drove this media
evolution. It establishes that early computer animation was not solely a
novel visual technique mimicking traditional drawing, but rather an
entirely new operational language for producing spatial and temporal
meaning---an architecture of operative ekphrasis where the descriptive
text (the code) literally *does* what it describes.

### Analytic Classification and the Identification of Bridges

The diverse outputs of the Bell Labs media ecology cannot be uniformly
collapsed under a single aesthetic or technical umbrella. A rigorous
media-archaeological approach requires assigning each source to specific
analytic categories that illuminate their distinct operational
functions, followed by identifying the specific technological or
institutional bridges that connect them.^1^

**Categorical Taxonomy:**

- **\<programming-language-for-animation\>**: Knowlton\'s 1964 paper on
  BEFLIX directly inhabits this space. It represents the foundational
  architectural logic of pixel manipulation and algorithmic subroutine
  formulation for the explicit purpose of moving image creation.^1^

- **\<simulation-film\> & \<scientific-visualization\>**: Zajac\'s 1963
  film and subsequent 1964/1965 papers inhabit this space. Crucially,
  his animations do not simulate reality for aesthetic purposes; they
  operate as mathematical plotting in temporal space to solve
  engineering problems regarding satellite orientation.^9^

- **\<gray-scale-image-synthesis\> & \<visual-perception-experiment\>**:
  The Harmon and Knowlton outputs (*Studies in Perception I*, 1972
  *Visible Language* paper) belong to this domain. Their primary
  objective was investigating the cognitive processing of binary output
  devices and the neurophysiology of pattern recognition.^4^

- **\<experimental-film\> & \<typographic-animation\>**: VanDerBeek and
  Knowlton's *Poemfield* series utilizes computational logic as an
  avant-garde poetic medium, manipulating language and geometric forms
  in temporal cinematic space.^6^

- **\<institutional-history\> & \<museum-contextualization\>**: The
  historical documentation provided by A. Michael Noll and the MoMA
  archival records of the 1968 *The Machine* exhibition fall under this
  category, explaining how corporate laboratories became accidental
  incubators for digital avant-gardism.^8^

**Identifying the Bridges:**

The institutional substrate of Bell Labs forced these distinct technical
vectors to intersect in profound ways.

1.  \<BEFLIX\> functions as the definitive bridge between
    \<programming-language-for-animation\> and \<experimental-film\>.
    Knowlton designed it for engineering visualization, but its
    underlying linguistic structure made it highly adaptable for
    VanDerBeek's poetic world-building.^2^

2.  \<Zajac\'s 1963 satellite film\> acts as the primary bridge between
    pure mathematics \<perspective-graphics\> and accessible
    \<scientific-visualization\>, proving that film could act as a
    translational layer between mainframe calculations and human
    comprehension.^3^

3.  \<Harmon-Knowlton gray-scale work\> bridges the stringent parameters
    of a \<visual-perception-experiment\> with the cultural impact of
    \<computer-art\>. What began as an inquiry into human optical
    resolution became a defining aesthetic artifact of the late 1960s,
    culminating in its exhibition at MoMA and broad cultural
    circulation.^5^

## Part II --- The Lineage Map of Computational Motion

The passage from raw electrical calculation to poetic computer cinema
was neither instantaneous nor accidental. It required a highly
sequential development of hardware interfaces, coding architectures,
perceptual theories, and cross-disciplinary interventions. The lineage
of executable cinema at Bell Labs can be explicitly mapped through the
following nodes of transition:

- **\<Bell Labs computation\>**: The baseline institutional substrate of
  the early 1960s, characterized by the use of IBM 7090 and 7094
  mainframe computers, massive punch-card data entry, and the need to
  process overwhelmingly complex mathematical models.^2^

- **\<programmed image\>**: The initial visualization phase. This
  includes Frank Sinden\'s ORBIT logic and A. Michael Noll\'s 1962/1963
  *Gaussian-Quadratic* experiments, where algorithms calculated Gaussian
  random numbers combined with quadratic equations to plot static
  vectors on microfilm.^3^

- **\<animated frame sequence\>**: The critical leap introduced by
  Knowlton\'s BEFLIX (1963/1964). Here, the computer transitions from
  plotting random static lines to mapping deliberate, sequential pixel
  operations across a defined 252x184 grid, automating the repetitive
  labor of animation.^1^

- **\<scientific simulation\>**: Concurrently in 1963, Zajac utilizes
  FORTRAN to calculate the dynamic physics of satellite gyroscopes,
  writing the sequential orientation data to a device that renders it as
  a continuous temporal simulation.^3^

- **\<computer-produced movie\>**: The crystallization of the medium.
  Knowlton defines the economic and technical viability of utilizing
  microfilm plotting (the SC-4020) and cathode-ray tubes to generate
  films without physical ink or paper, as outlined in his 1965 *Science*
  article.^1^

- **\<gray-scale perception image\>**: Harmon and Knowlton (1966/1967)
  push the hardware beyond high-contrast vectors. They convert analog
  voltages of photographs into discrete binary numbers, using
  typographic characters to simulate tonal depth and test human
  perceptual limits.^4^

- **\<experimental computer film\>**: The aesthetic culmination
  (1966--1971). VanDerBeek and Knowlton compile \"worldtext\" and
  optical color into the *Poemfields*, utilizing the machine\'s
  intrinsic capabilities for text and logic to create psychedelic,
  kinetic poetry.^6^

- **\<museum / art-historical canonization\>**: The institutional
  validation. MoMA\'s 1968 exhibition *The Machine as Seen at the End of
  the Mechanical Age*, paired with E.A.T. collaborations, permanently
  positions Bell Labs\' executable code within the narrative of art
  history.^5^

## Part III --- Research Brief: Synthesizing the Media-Archaeological Lineage

The transformation of mainframe computer technology into an engine for
moving-image production represents one of the most critical thresholds
in modern media archaeology. The primary and secondary sources gathered
from the 1963--1968 Bell Labs era reveal a highly coherent,
interconnected lineage. This history demonstrates how early computer
animation transcended simple visual novelty to establish an entirely new
operational language that would dictate the future of digital
simulation, data visualization, and computational art.

### Computation as Image-Making and Programming as Cinematic Instruction

The genesis of this lineage is inherently tied to the rigid hardware
constraints and limited graphical capabilities of the early 1960s. Prior
to the targeted interventions of Kenneth Knowlton, computer graphics
were largely restricted to vector-based line plotting---the calculation
of starting and ending points for an electron beam to trace simple
geometric forms on an oscilloscope or plotting device, as seen in A.
Michael Noll\'s 1962 *Gaussian-Quadratic* work.^8^

In 1963, Kenneth Knowlton introduced a paradigm shift by developing
BEFLIX (Bell Flicks). Recognized today as the first embedded
domain-specific language explicitly developed for computer animation,
BEFLIX fundamentally altered the relationship between the machine and
the image.^1^ Knowlton shifted the paradigm from continuous vector lines
to a discrete bitmap logic. With BEFLIX, programming was transformed
into cinematic instruction. The animator or engineer did not manually
draw frames; rather, they wrote a sequential series of subroutines using
FORTRAN and FORTRAN Assembly Program (FAP) macros that dictated the
specific behavior of pixels.^2^

As rigorously detailed in his 1964 paper, \"A Computer Technique for
Producing Animated Movies\" (presented at the AFIPS Spring Joint
Computer Conference), BEFLIX allowed programmers to manipulate an array
of microscopic cells within a fixed resolution matrix of 252 by 184.^1^
Each of these localized cells could display one of eight distinct shades
of gray.^1^ This operational shift from analog drawing to digital coding
meant that the movie became, for the first time, an executable file. The
IBM 7090 or 7094 mainframe computer interpreted the alphanumeric script,
constructed the pictures mathematically within its memory, and wrote the
rendering coordinates onto a magnetic tape.^2^

This magnetic tape was then fed into the Stromberg-Carlson (SC) 4020
microfilm recorder.^2^ Inside the SC-4020, a cathode-ray tube read the
tape, tracing the image with an electron beam. An automated, shutterless
16mm camera mounted inside a darkroom enclosure then captured the
sequence frame-by-frame as it flashed on the CRT face.^9^ BEFLIX treated
the cinematic frame not as a photographic exposure capturing the
physical bounce of light off the material world, but as a manipulable
data structure---a grid awaiting algorithmic population.^2^ Knowlton
would continue to refine this operational ekphrasis by developing
subsequent graphics programming languages throughout his career at Bell
Labs, including EXPLOR, SPHERES, and ATOMS, further proving the
viability of language as an image generator.^12^

### Simulation as Visual Explanation: Zajac and the Architecture of the Cosmos

While Knowlton was architecting the programming language necessary for
bitmap motion, his colleague Edward E. Zajac was pioneering the use of
computer graphics to solve critical aerospace engineering problems. In
1963, Zajac produced what is universally recognized as the first
computer-animated scientific film: *Simulation of a Two-Gyro
Gravity-Gradient Attitude Control System*.^3^ To fully grasp the
historical and epistemological impact of Zajac\'s work, it is imperative
not to confuse it with mere artistic expression or aesthetic curiosity;
it was a highly pragmatic solution to a complex mathematical bottleneck.

During this period, Zajac and his colleague J.A. Lewis were deep into an
analytical and numerical study of passive gravity-gradient attitude
control systems for communications satellites.^11^ The goal was to
determine how a satellite---specifically a 300-lb satellite in a
6000-nautical-mile orbit with a 60-foot extensible rod and a 20-lb tip
mass---could maintain an Earth-pointing orientation using only the
physical forces of gravity and gyroscopic energy dissipation.^11^
Gravity-gradient systems are inherently \"bistable,\" meaning they can
easily settle into an inverted, upside-down equilibrium if disturbed by
orbital eccentricity, thermal rod bending, solar torques, magnetic
torques, or micrometeorite impact.^11^

The differential equations governing this multidimensional, non-linear
dynamic system were processed by the IBM mainframes, but the output was
an overwhelming, unreadable flood of thousands of pages of numerical
data representing the transient response and yaw, pitch, and roll
axes.^9^ Engineers could not intuitively \"see\" if the satellite was
tumbling or stabilizing by reading printouts.^9^

By programming the orientation calculations in FORTRAN and utilizing a
geometric plotting sub-routine called ORBIT---written by his colleague
Frank Sinden---Zajac mathematically calculated a perspective view of the
satellite.^3^ He plotted the satellite\'s orientation as a series of
bounding boxes directly onto the SC-4020 microfilm recorder.^3^ In his
influential 1964 paper, \"Computer-Made Perspective Movies as a
Scientific and Communication Tool,\" and his subsequent 1965 article in
the SMPTE Journal, Zajac explicitly framed the resulting movie as an
essential scientific tool.^9^ Zajac's profound innovation was
recognizing that the motion picture medium could serve as a time-based
visual explanation for transient, multi-body physical phenomena. The
film was the only way to quickly prove that their two-gyro damper system
would settle small disturbances within a single orbit.^11^ Zajac
demonstrated that simulation film was an epistemological necessity,
turning pure mathematics into a visible physical reality.^9^

### Perceptual Synthesis and Gray Scales: The Computer as Cognitive Mirror

While Zajac visualized macro-physical space and celestial mechanics,
Leon Harmon and Kenneth Knowlton turned the computational lens inward,
focusing on the psychophysics of human vision. Their 1966/1967 artifact,
*Computer Nude (Studies in Perception I)*, represents a profound bridge
between visual perception experiments and the nascent field of digital
art.^5^

To produce this iconic image, Harmon (a cognitive neuroscience
researcher) and Knowlton scanned a photograph of the avant-garde
choreographer Deborah Hay. They utilized a special camera setup that
converted the continuous analog voltages of the photograph into discrete
binary numbers.^5^ These binary numbers were then algorithmically
assigned to standard typographic symbols---such as letters, numbers, and
mathematical marks---based on their specific, calculated halftone
densities.^13^ By quantizing the spatial layout into discrete
microscopic cells and applying rules to mimic subjective gray values,
Harmon and Knowlton engineered a massive mosaic (the physical photoprint
measured 150 by 370 cm).^12^

As extensively detailed in their 1972 paper in *Visible Language*,
\"Computer-Produced Grey Scales,\" this process was initially conceived
as an extension of cognitive neuroscience research. They sought to
understand how pictorial output from computers, which is
\"microscopically black or white at a point owing to the physical
processes involved,\" could be perceived by the human brain as having
depth, curve, and continuous tone.^4^ It raised critical \"intriguing
questions about the physiology and psychology of vision\".^4^

However, the artifact rapidly transcended the confines of the
laboratory. Through the advocacy of the non-profit group Experiments in
Art and Technology (E.A.T.)---founded by engineers Billy Klüver and Fred
Waldhauer alongside artists Robert Rauschenberg and Robert Whitman---the
image was brought into the public sphere. Rauschenberg famously hung a
copy of the 12-foot print in his studio during an E.A.T. press
conference.^5^ Consequently, on October 11, 1967, the image was
reproduced in *The New York Times*, inadvertently becoming the
publication\'s first full-frontal nude.^5^ While Bell Labs management
initially demanded that the researchers dissociate the laboratory\'s
name from the controversial work, the overwhelmingly positive public
reception caused the corporation to reverse its directive, proudly
claiming the innovation.^33^ The gray-scale project demonstrated that
the computer image is fundamentally an illusion of spatial
quantization---a mosaic that relies heavily on the neurobiology of human
perception to resolve rigid algorithmic symbols into soft, organic
flesh.^5^

### BEFLIX as Worldtext and the Move to Experimental Film

The functional capabilities established by Knowlton\'s BEFLIX and the
aesthetic breakthroughs of perceptual gray-scale manipulation culminated
in a radical synthesis when Knowlton began collaborating with the
avant-garde filmmaker Stan VanDerBeek. From 1966 to 1971, the duo
produced the highly influential *Poemfield* series (comprising films
numbered 1 through 10).^6^

In these collaborative films, the computer screen became what can be
termed a \"worldtext.\" Using BEFLIX alongside a specialized macro
system created by Knowlton specifically for this collaboration, known as
TARPS, VanDerBeek wrote programmatic instructions that produced complex,
multilayered moving tapestries of geometric graphics intertwined with
cascading typography.^6^ Rather than using the computer to simulate
physical reality (as Zajac did with satellites) or to reproduce a
photographic representation (as Harmon did with the human form), the
*Poemfield* films utilized the intrinsic, native nature of the
computer\'s memory banks: alphanumeric text and pure logic.^6^

Words and letters dissolve, reform, twinkle, and mutate across the
frame. Because the SC-4020 microfilm recorder could only output imagery
in stark black and white or varying shades of microfilm gray-scale, the
psychedelic, vibrant color palettes associated with the final
*Poemfields* were not generated natively by the IBM 7094.^8^ Instead,
VanDerBeek employed complex optical printing techniques, collaborating
heavily with experimental filmmakers Bob Brown and Frank Olvey, to
colorize the raw algorithmic output in post-production.^7^

The poetry within the films was equally significant. In *Poemfield No.
7* (1967-68), for example, VanDerBeek programmed a poem by the
nonviolent social activist A.J. Muste, which declared: \"THERE IS NO WAY
TO PEACE; PEACE IS THE WAY; NO MORE WAR\".^6^ This text was combined
with a soundtrack featuring movements from John Cage\'s composition
*Amores*.^6^ Here, the motion picture is fully realized as operative
ekphrasis---a linguistic script that executes an immersive, abstract
visual environment. VanDerBeek effectively hacked the corporate machine,
utilizing Knowlton\'s algorithms as an \"image-based poetry language\"
to deliver coded, political messages through the very technology
developed for Cold War engineering.^6^ It represented a liberation of
the machine, fulfilling VanDerBeek\'s vision that one could eventually
\"sit someplace in a railroad station and write a movie\".^7^

### Bell Labs as Institutional Substrate and the Canonization of Code

None of these diverse innovations---the satellite simulations, the
bitmap languages, the perceptual nudes, or the typographic
poetry---occurred in a vacuum. They were the direct, manifest result of
the unique institutional ecosystem of Bell Telephone Laboratories.^1^ As
noted by A. Michael Noll in his historical analyses of early digital
art, the environment at Bell Labs during the 1960s allowed for an
unprecedented, heavily resourced cross-pollination between
telecommunications engineering, acoustical research, computer science,
and artistic inquiry.^8^

Engineers provided the necessary technical scaffolding. Frank Sinden
provided the ORBIT plotting logic ^3^; Noll established the parameters
for generative patterns and later pursued 3D stereoscopic and haptic
virtual reality systems ^34^; and Knowlton provided the robust BEFLIX
and EXPLOR languages.^12^ The institution\'s increasingly open-door
policy for artists---facilitated largely by the E.A.T. network---allowed
figures like VanDerBeek and Lillian Schwartz (who arrived as a
\"resident visitor\" in 1968 and stayed for decades) to explore the
outer limits of computational media without needing to possess advanced
degrees in mathematics.^7^

The ultimate cultural validation of this era of executable cinema
arrived on November 21, 1968, inside the walls of the Museum of Modern
Art (MoMA) in New York City.^26^ During MoMA\'s landmark, epoch-defining
exhibition, *The Machine as Seen at the End of the Mechanical Age*,
curated by Pontus Hultén, Knowlton was invited to host a screening
titled \"An Evening of Computer-Produced Films\".^26^ Presenting works
born entirely from the logic circuits of Bell Labs, Boeing, and Lawrence
Radiation Laboratory to an elite art-historical audience, this event
explicitly and permanently shifted the cultural framing of computer
animation. It moved from being strictly a \"scientific and educational
tool\" (which was Zajac\'s original, pragmatic mandate) into the domain
of legitimate, autonomous fine art.^9^ *Studies in Perception I* was
featured heavily in the exhibition, proving that the algorithm was now
fully integrated into the narrative of 20th-century aesthetic
development.^13^

## Part IV --- Open Questions and Trajectories for Future Media-Archaeological Research

While the primary source record establishes a clear, chronological
lineage of early computer cinema at Bell Labs, this historical cluster
remains dense with complexities. Several critical questions remain
unresolved, requiring further primary archival research, software
excavation, and frame-by-frame film analysis. Addressing these open
questions is vital for scholars looking to fully map the architecture of
1960s computational media.

**1. The Exact Mechanics of Motion Encoding in BEFLIX:** While the
literature clearly defines BEFLIX as an embedded domain-specific
language operating in a rigid 252x184 cartesian grid ^1^, the precise
syntactical architecture of how motion operations were actually encoded
remains poorly documented in accessible histories. Without modern memory
buffers or frame-rendering software, how exactly did Knowlton encode
temporal transitions---such as pans, zooms, dissolves, or sprite
logic---using pure text? Retrieving the original FORTRAN II and FAP
macro source codes is necessary to clarify how early algorithmic
efficiency was achieved within the strict memory limits of the IBM
7094.^2^

**2. The Direct Influence of Zajac on Knowlton's Software
Architecture:** Zajac produced the first simulation film in 1963,
relying on FORTRAN and the ORBIT sub-routine to draw lines. Knowlton
developed the bitmap-based BEFLIX concurrently in 1963, publishing his
results in 1964.^1^ It is necessary to determine the exact collaborative
mechanics between these two researchers. Did Knowlton design BEFLIX
explicitly as a response to solve the clunky animation difficulties
Zajac encountered with FORTRAN vector plotting? Or did the two projects
evolve in relatively isolated silos within the massive Bell Labs
complex, representing parallel rather than sequential evolution?

**3. The Extent of TARPS vs. BEFLIX in the Poemfields:** Archival
sources indicate that VanDerBeek utilized both BEFLIX and a subsequent,
specialized macro language called TARPS, which Knowlton wrote
specifically for the filmmaker.^6^ A granular, frame-by-frame technical
analysis of the *Poemfields* mapped against Knowlton\'s surviving code
archives is required. Scholars must definitively catalog which specific
visual effects (e.g., standard text generation, pixel cascading,
geometric morphing) were native to the baseline BEFLIX architecture, and
which required the bespoke TARPS macros.^8^ This will reveal exactly how
much the software had to be mutated to accommodate artistic intent.

**4. The Cultural Permeability of the Laboratory Substrate:** How deeply
did Bell Labs' scientific and corporate culture shape the aesthetics of
its computer art? While VanDerBeek pushed for anti-war messaging (as
seen in *Poemfield No. 7*) ^6^, the tools he used were funded by a
corporate monopoly deeply tied to the military-industrial complex.
Further research must interrogate the friction between the laboratory\'s
primary function (telecommunications and defense research) and the
avant-garde, anti-establishment messaging produced on its machines. The
initial pushback against Harmon\'s *Computer Nude* highlights this
tension, yet the laboratory ultimately embraced the PR value of these
works.^33^

**5. Distribution, Screening, and Archival Fidelity:** Because the
*Poemfields* relied heavily on Bob Brown and Frank Olvey's
post-computation optical colorization ^7^, determining the definitive
\"original\" state of these films is exceptionally complex. Further
investigation is needed into how these films were distributed and
exhibited in the 1960s (e.g., within VanDerBeek\'s immersive
*Moviedrome* or multiscreen installations like *Cine Dreams*).^7^
Moreover, do distinct optical color iterations exist for the exact same
underlying BEFLIX code sequence? Modern archiving efforts must carefully
separate the raw, black-and-white SC-4020 microfilm output from the
analog optical post-production applied by the filmmakers.

**6. The Divergence in Technical Vocabulary:** A rigorous semantic
analysis is required to trace exactly when and why Knowlton's specific
terminology of \"computer-produced movies\" ^1^ transitioned into the
universally adopted phrase \"computer animation,\" as championed by
Zajac in his 1965 and 1966 publications.^9^ Understanding this
linguistic shift will provide insight into how the field migrated from
viewing the computer as an automated, mechanical manufacturer of
traditional film frames to recognizing it as a native, autonomous medium
of animated expression.

By addressing these open questions, future scholarship can continue to
unearth how the unique institutional culture of Bell Laboratories
functioned as the vital crucible for transforming mathematical
calculation into the modern visual regime of digital media. The legacy
of BEFLIX, Zajac\'s satellites, and the *Poemfields* proves that the
computer was never simply a new kind of camera; it was a new kind of
language, capable of executing the visual world into existence.
