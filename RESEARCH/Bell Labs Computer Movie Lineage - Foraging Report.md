# Part II: Lineage Map

The progression of computer movie work at Bell Telephone Laboratories
represents a transformative shift from purely mathematical calculation
to expressive cinematic motion. This lineage is defined by four
evolutionary stages:

1.  **Foundational Simulation (1963--1964):** The genesis occurs with
    Edward Zajac's work on satellite dynamics. By translating the
    complex math of \"Two-gyro satellite attitude control\" [[(Lewis &
    Zajac
    1964)]{.underline}](https://www.content-animation.org.uk/htmls/lewis.htm)
    into a visual \"Simulation of a Two-Gyro Gravity-Gradient Attitude
    Control System\" [[(Zajac
    1963)]{.underline}](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y-FaCJs1UEc),
    the computer was first utilized as a medium for \"visual
    explanation.\"

2.  **Procedural Language Construction (1964--1965):** Kenneth Knowlton
    expanded this capability by creating
    [[BEFLIX]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BEFLIX), a
    specialized language for computer animation. This transitioned the
    field from \"one-off\" simulations to a general-purpose \"A Computer
    Technique for Producing Animated Movies\" [[(Knowlton
    1964)]{.underline}](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/45/2017/09/moviemaking-knowlton.pdf).

3.  **Perceptual Synthesis and Gray Scales (1967--1977):** The lineage
    moved toward understanding the \"computational image\" through the
    lens of human psychology. Harmon and Knowlton's \"Studies in
    Perception\"
    [[(1967)]{.underline}](https://buffaloakg.org/artworks/p20142-computer-nude-studies-perception-i)
    used computer-processed tiles to explore how the brain synthesizes
    information, later formalized in their research on
    \"Computer-Produced Grey Scales\" [[(Harmon & Knowlton
    1977)]{.underline}](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5226).

4.  **Aesthetic and Poetic Experimentation (1966--1969):** The lineage
    culminates in the collaboration between Knowlton and Stan VanDerBeek
    on the
    [[Poemfield]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poem_Field)
    series. Here, the technical tools of BEFLIX were applied to
    \"experimental film,\" blending concrete poetry with procedural
    graphics to create a new form of digital art.

# Part III: Research Brief

The development of computer-generated movies at Bell Telephone
Laboratories during the 1960s serves as a critical media-archaeological
lineage, marking the moment when computation was first reimagined as a
medium for image-making. This evolution did not occur in isolation but
was the result of a unique institutional substrate provided by Bell
Labs, where technical precision met speculative inquiry (Noll 2016).

## Computation as Image-Making and Simulation

The lineage began with the necessity of simulation. Edward Zajac's 1963
film, \"Simulation of a Two-Gyro Gravity-Gradient Attitude Control
System,\" transformed raw mathematical data into a \"visual
explanation\" [[(Zajac
1963)]{.underline}](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y-FaCJs1UEc). Before
this, complex satellite dynamics were communicated through static charts
and equations. Zajac argued that computer-made perspective movies were
essential \"scientific and communication tools\" (Zajac 1964), allowing
researchers to witness the physical implications of their calculations.
This established the concept of the computational image not as a mere
decoration, but as an essential cognitive bridge.

## Programming as Cinematic Instruction: BEFLIX

The bridge from individual simulation to a broader cinematic medium was
constructed by Kenneth Knowlton through the development of the BEFLIX
language. BEFLIX allowed for \"executable cinema\"---a system where
\"programming as cinematic instruction\" replaced traditional cel
animation. As detailed in his 1964 paper, \"A Computer Technique for
Producing Animated Movies,\" Knowlton created a way to describe
\"animated bitmap worlds\" through macro-instructions [(Knowlton
1964)](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/45/2017/09/moviemaking-knowlton.pdf).
This was an \"operative ekphrasis,\" where the computer code acted as a
descriptive script that the machine then realized as visual frames.
BEFLIX turned the computer into a \"worldtext,\" where every pixel could
be manipulated via logical operators.

## Gray Scale and Perceptual Synthesis

As the lineage matured, focus shifted from the generation of images to
the perception of them. Harmon and Knowlton's \"Studies in Perception,\"
famously known as the \"Computer Nude,\" utilized a grid of varied grey
tones to challenge human visual recognition (Harmon & Knowlton 1967).
This work demonstrated that the computational image could be a
\"perceptual synthesis,\" where discrete digital data (grey scales)
could be integrated by the human eye into a coherent whole [(Harmon &
Knowlton 1977)](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5226).
This research was fundamental in understanding how computer graphics
could effectively communicate information to a human viewer.

## From Technical Demonstration to Experimental Film

The final movement in this lineage is the transition from \"technical
demonstration\" to \"experimental film.\" The collaboration between
Kenneth Knowlton and Stan VanDerBeek on the Poemfield films (1966--1969)
represent the artistic maturation of the Bell Labs tools. Using the
logic of BEFLIX, these films explored the intersection of language,
rhythm, and procedural imagery. By this stage, the computer was no
longer just a calculator of satellite orbits; it was a poetic instrument
capable of generating \"computational image languages\" that resonated
with the avant-garde cinema of the era [(MoMA
2011)](https://www.moma.org/calendar/events/4117).

Ultimately, the Bell Labs lineage shows that computer animation began as
a form of simulation but rapidly evolved into a sophisticated language
of motion. It proved that the computer was not merely a tool for solving
equations, but a generative engine for creating entire visual worlds
through the logic of code.

# Part IV: Open Questions

1.  How did the hardware constraints of the IBM 7090 and the
    Stromberg-Carlson 4020 microfilm recorder fundamentally dictate the
    aesthetic \"grain\" of the BEFLIX language compared to later digital
    systems?

2.  To what extent did Edward Zajac's early \"perspective movies\"
    influence the broader 1960s educational film movement beyond the
    confines of Bell Telephone Laboratories?

3.  In the \"Studies in Perception\" experiments, what specific
    algorithmic thresholds were identified as the minimum requirements
    for a human to synthesize a \"grey scale\" image into a recognizable
    object?

4.  What is the current state of preservation for the original BEFLIX
    source code and the magnetic tapes used to generate the
    [[Poemfield]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poem_Field)
    series?

5.  How did the institutional culture of Bell Labs---specifically the
    lack of commercial pressure for these projects---allow for the
    unique transition from scientific \"simulation\" to \"experimental
    art\"?
